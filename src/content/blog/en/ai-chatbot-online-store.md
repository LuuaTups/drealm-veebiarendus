In an online store, a customer often has one question standing between them and the checkout. Does this jacket run small or large? Can this cream be used on sensitive skin? When will the parcel reach Tartu? If the answer doesn’t come right away, they close the tab. After the purchase come new questions: where is my order, and how do I return this?

We wrote about chatbots in general in [is an AI chatbot right for my business](/en/blog/is-an-ai-chatbot-right-for-my-business). Here we look specifically at online stores: which questions a bot can take over, when it pays off and how to avoid a bot telling a customer something wrong.

## Which questions an online store chatbot handles

Online store questions fall roughly into four groups.

**Product questions before buying.** Material, dimensions, ingredients, fit, care, whether one product works with another. The answers are usually on the product page, but the customer can’t find them or doesn’t want to read it all. The bot finds the right information and answers briefly.

**Size and choice help.** The customer describes what they’re looking for and the bot suggests suitable products from your range. For clothing and footwear, the bot can use your size chart and product notes, for example that a certain model runs smaller than usual.

**Order status.** When the bot is connected to your store, it can say whether an order has been received, packed or shipped, and share the tracking link. Otherwise this is a question that lands in your customer service inbox.

**Returns, exchanges and delivery.** The return deadline, how to send a product back, what delivery costs and how long it takes. These answers come from your terms of sale.

## Estonian, Russian and English

Not every customer of an Estonian online store speaks Estonian. If your product information exists only in Estonian, some customers are left alone with their question. An AI chatbot can reply in the customer’s language even when your product information is written in one language.

There’s an important caveat. Accuracy can get lost in translation, especially with sizes, ingredients and terms. So it’s worth writing the most important information, such as size charts and return terms, in every language your customers use, and checking during the first weeks how the bot phrases those answers.

## When an online store chatbot pays off

A bot pays off when:

- customers often ask the same product questions before buying;
- “where is my order” and return questions take customer service time every day;
- you have a large range and customers need help choosing;
- customers write in the evening and at weekends when nobody replies;
- you have customers in several languages.

It doesn’t pay off when questions are few, when product information is incomplete or out of date, or when every purchase needs long personal advice. In that case, fix your product pages and FAQ first. That helps both your customers and any chatbot you add later.

To judge whether it pays off, look at your customer service inbox. How many questions arrive each month, how many are repeats and how much time goes into answering them? If a large share are questions whose answers are already written down, a chatbot has clear work to do.

## How to avoid wrong answers

In an online store a wrong answer costs more than on an ordinary website. If the bot says a product is in stock when it isn’t, or promises a return your terms don’t allow, that is already a promise to the customer. These principles reduce the risk.

**1. Answers only from your information.** The bot answers from product pages, size charts, the FAQ and your terms of sale. It doesn’t use general knowledge about how other stores do things.

**2. Stock, price and order status come straight from the store.** This data changes all the time. The bot must not remember or guess it, but fetch it from the store every time.

**3. For returns and warranty, only the wording of your terms.** The bot explains the terms but doesn’t make exceptions or promise refunds. For example, goods bought online in the EU generally come with a 14-day right of withdrawal, but your terms may contain additional conditions. The bot should repeat your terms, not invent new ones.

**4. A clear line for handing over to a person.** Complaints, damaged goods, exceptions, large orders and anything where information is missing go to a person. The bot collects contact details and describes the issue so the customer doesn’t have to explain it again.

**5. Customers know they’re talking to a machine.** The EU AI Act requires that people are made aware when they interact with an AI system such as a chatbot. Say so clearly at the start of the conversation.

**6. Regular review.** Go through the conversations during the first weeks. Where the bot struggled, something is usually missing from your product information or terms. Fix the source, not just the bot.

## What to get in order before you start

- **Product pages.** Material, dimensions, ingredients and care need to be written down. A bot can’t answer from information that doesn’t exist.
- **Size charts.** One chart per product group, plus notes on models that differ.
- **Terms of sale.** Delivery, returns, exchanges and warranty in plain language.
- **FAQ.** Write down the questions customer service gets most often.
- **Store connection.** For order status and stock levels the bot needs access to your store data. On common platforms such as Shopify and WooCommerce this is possible.

## Where to start

1. **Pick two or three topics,** for example product questions and order status.
2. **Connect the bot to your store** and give it your product information and terms.
3. **Test it with your team** before going live. Ask awkward and difficult questions too.
4. **Launch and monitor:** how many conversations there were, how many went to a person and where the bot struggled.
5. **Expand** to further topics once the first ones work.
