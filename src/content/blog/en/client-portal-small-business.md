In many small businesses, client information moves between emails, files and phone calls. A client asks where their order is, wants last month’s invoice sent again or can’t find the contract you sent three months ago. Each question is small, but together they take up a noticeable part of the working day.

A client portal is a web space where clients log in and see their information themselves. Below we look at what it does, who it suits and how to start so that the first version is useful without taking on much risk.

## What a client portal does

A client portal isn’t one fixed product. It’s a place where each client sees exactly what concerns them. Depending on the business, it can include:

- **Documents and files.** Contracts, quotes, reports, drawings, certificates. Clients find them on their own and you don’t have to send them again.
- **Orders and their status.** What has been ordered, what is on the way and what has arrived.
- **Invoices and payments.** Open and paid invoices in one place.
- **Project status.** Stages, deadlines and what is expected from the client.
- **Requests and messages.** Clients can submit a new request or question so that it reaches the right person and the history is kept.
- **File uploads.** Clients can send documents, photos or data you need for your work.

A good client portal doesn’t try to do everything. It does the few things clients need most often, and does them well.

## Who it suits

A client portal is most useful for businesses with regular clients and recurring contact. For example:

- accounting and advisory firms, where clients send documents and wait for reports;
- construction and renovation companies, where clients want to see project progress and drawings;
- wholesalers and manufacturers whose business customers reorder regularly;
- maintenance providers, where clients want to see service history and the next visit;
- trainers and consultants who share materials.

If your relationship with a client is one-off, for example you sell a product and that’s it, a portal adds less. A good website and a clear contact form will serve you better.

## Signs a portal is worth considering

- **Clients keep asking for the same information.** Where is my order? Has the invoice been paid? When will the work be done?
- **Files travel by email.** The right version is hard to find and things sometimes go missing.
- **Information lives in one person’s head or inbox.** When that person is on holiday, nobody knows the answer.
- **Clients want information outside office hours.** They shouldn’t have to wait for someone to reply.
- **You use several tools that don’t talk to each other.** Orders in one place, invoices in another and messages in a third.

If several of these sound familiar, part of your team’s time goes into work a portal could take over.

## Off-the-shelf software or a custom build

Before building a custom portal, check whether the software you already use has a client view. Many accounting, project management and CRM tools include one. If it meets your needs, start there.

A custom portal makes sense when your process differs from what off-the-shelf software assumes, when information sits in several systems and needs to be brought together, or when you want the portal to look and work like part of your own service.

## What drives the cost

The cost of a client portal depends on scope and integrations. The main questions are:

- how many different things clients see and do in the portal;
- whether information comes from existing systems such as accounting, inventory or a CRM, and how many integrations are needed;
- how many user roles you need, for example the client’s manager, the client’s staff and your own team;
- whether the portal needs more than one language;
- how much of an admin view your own team needs.

## Security and access

A client portal holds data that must not end up in the wrong hands. The Estonian Data Protection Inspectorate’s guidance on data security stresses that everyone should have access only to the data they are permitted to see, and that it must be possible to trace afterwards who accessed what. In practice this means:

- **Each client sees only their own information.** Permissions are checked on every request, not just at login.
- **Roles are clear.** A client company can have several users with different rights.
- **Secure login.** A strong password and, where needed, a second step such as a code by email or from an authenticator app.
- **Logs.** You can see who logged in when and what they changed.
- **Collect only what you need.** The less personal data in the portal, the lower the risk.

## Where to start

1. **Find the most frequent question or task.** The thing clients ask about most, or that you most often send by hand.
2. **Build the first version around it.** For example, only documents and invoices, or only order status.
3. **Pick a few regular clients** who are willing to try the first version and give feedback.
4. **Connect your existing systems** so information reaches the portal automatically, not by hand.
5. **Expand** once the first part works and clients are using it.

Clients start using a portal when it’s easier than sending an email. That’s why the first version has to be very simple and do one thing well.

## Summary

A client portal gives clients one place to see their documents, orders, invoices or project status. For a small business it helps when clients keep asking for the same information and files travel by email. Start with the most frequent need, keep the first version simple and connect it to the systems you already use.
