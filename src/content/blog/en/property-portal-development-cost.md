A real estate portal is more than a website with lots of listings. It has two very different users: the buyer or tenant looking for a home, and the advertiser who wants to publish a listing fast and receive enquiries. A portal succeeds when both find it easy. If one side struggles, the other doesn't grow either.

Below we break down what a portal consists of, which decisions affect the price most, and how to start without spending money on things you don't need on day one.

## What a property portal needs

**Search and filters.** This is the heart of the portal. Buyers want to filter by price, size, rooms, floor and location and see results instantly, not after a page reload. If search is slow or filters don't work together, visitors leave.

**Map view.** In real estate, location often matters more than price. On a map, people see at a glance what is near a school, workplace or the sea. The map must work on phones and with many listings.

**Listing page.** Photos, floor plan, key details, location and a clear enquiry button. Every listing page is also a landing page for Google, so it needs a proper title, description and structured data.

**Advertiser accounts.** Agents or private sellers add, edit and remove listings themselves. Adding a listing should take minutes: drag in photos, fill in a form, change status with one click.

**Enquiries and favourites.** Buyers save interesting listings and ask for information without opening their email. The enquiry goes straight to the listing’s agent.

**Admin.** The portal owner sees users, listings, enquiries and payments in one place.

## Where the cost comes from

A portal’s price doesn’t depend on the number of pages but on the logic. Five decisions affect it most.

**1. Who adds listings.** If only one agency adds listings, the system is simple. If the portal is open to all agents and private sellers, you need sign-up, verification, permissions and support. That is significantly more work.

**2. Where listings come from.** Manual entry is simplest. If listings come automatically from another system (via an XML feed or API), you need an import that validates, updates and removes data. A well-built import saves a lot of manual work later.

**3. Whether listings are paid.** Paid listings and featured placements need payments, invoices and rules for what happens when a period ends. If the business model depends on this, plan it from the start.

**4. Languages.** Each extra language means a translated interface, listing descriptions and separate SEO. In Estonia you often need at least Estonian, English and Russian.

**5. Listing volume.** Ten listings and ten thousand listings are technically different tasks. Search, images and the map must be built so speed doesn’t drop as the portal grows.

A simple portal showing one agency’s listings can be built in a few weeks. An open portal with many advertisers, payments and integrations is a project of several months. ## How to start the right way

The most common mistake is trying to build everything big portals have, right away. It’s better to start with the smallest version that solves the core problem:

1. **Write down the core action.** For example: “a buyer finds a suitable apartment and sends an enquiry” or “an agent adds a listing in five minutes”.
2. **Build the first version around it.** Search, listing page, enquiry and simple admin.
3. **Let real users try it.** See where they get stuck.
4. **Add features based on usage,** not assumptions.

That way the portal reaches users in weeks, not months, and every further euro goes into something people actually need.

## SEO: why every listing must be findable on Google

Many property searches start on Google: “3-room apartment in Kalamaja”, “house for rent in Tartu”. If listing and area pages are properly built, every new listing can bring new traffic. You need:

- a unique URL and title for each listing;
- area and category pages that group listings;
- structured data so Google understands price, location and type;
- fast loading even with many photos.

A sold or removed listing page shouldn’t just disappear. Redirect it to similar listings or the area page so accumulated visibility isn’t lost.

## Summary

A property portal succeeds when search is fast, adding listings is easy and every listing is findable on Google. Price is driven mostly by who adds listings, where they come from, whether they’re paid, how many languages you need and how big the portal will grow.
