There are many website builders and quotes can differ several times over. The cheapest quote isn’t always bad and the most expensive isn’t always good. The difference usually lies in what’s included and what’s left out. These ten questions help you compare quotes and avoid unpleasant surprises.

## 1. Who owns the finished website?

This is the most important question. Domain, hosting, code, content and access should be in your name. If the developer keeps them, you’re tied to them even when the collaboration no longer works.

## 2. What exactly does the price include?

Ask in writing: how many pages, how many languages, whether copy, images, SEO, analytics and a contact form are included. “Websites from €500” can mean very different things.

## 3. Is the price fixed?

An hourly project can drag on and get more expensive. A fixed price for an agreed scope gives certainty. Also ask how changes during the project are handled.

## 4. Who writes the copy?

Copy is often the biggest time sink and the biggest reason projects stall. If you have to write everything yourself, factor that in. A good developer helps with copy or writes it with you.

## 5. How is SEO handled?

At minimum ask: does every page have its own title and description, is the site fast on phones, is there a sitemap and Search Console setup. If you’re replacing an old site, always ask how old URLs will be redirected.

## 6. What technology will be used and why?

You don’t need to be a technical expert. It’s enough that the developer can explain simply why this solution suits you and what it means for maintenance, speed and security.

## 7. Can I edit content myself?

If you plan to add news, products or blog posts often, you need an admin. If the site changes rarely, it may make more sense for the developer to make changes.

## 8. What happens after launch?

Ask who fixes bugs, who updates the tech and what small changes cost. A clear agreement prevents every small thing from turning into a negotiation.

## 9. Can I see previous work?

Look at real work, not just design mockups. Open it on a phone, try the contact form and see if it loads fast. If possible, ask a client how the collaboration went.

## 10. Who is my contact person?

In a smaller studio you usually talk directly to the person doing the work. A larger agency may have a project manager. Both work; what matters is knowing who to contact, how, and how quickly they reply.

## Summary

Compare quotes on content, not just price. The most important points are ownership, what the price includes, who writes the copy, SEO and what happens after launch. If a developer answers these clearly and in writing, the collaboration is likely to go well.
